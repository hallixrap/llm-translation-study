Evaluating LLM Translation Fidelity for Medical Documents

Across High- and Low-Resource Languages

The Question

Can LLMs reliably translate health education materials across diverse languages, including low-resource languages with limited training data?

We evaluated four frontier LLMs on their ability to translate real medical documents (vaccine information sheets and cancer education materials) into 8 languages spanning high-resource to low-resource categories, comparing them against professional human translations.

The Key Finding

Low-resource languages achieve semantic fidelity comparable to high-resource languages. Tagalog (LaBSE: 0.950) and Haitian Creole (0.955)—languages with <0.01% CommonCrawl representation—achieve scores indistinguishable from Spanish (0.954) and Vietnamese (0.953). LLMs preserve medical meaning equally well regardless of training data availability.

Figure: All languages achieve comparable semantic fidelity (Panel A), while lexical overlap varies by linguistic factors (Panel B).

The distinction matters: semantic fidelity (LaBSE) measures whether the meaning is preserved—what patients need. Lexical fidelity (BLEU) measures word choice overlap with professional translations—less critical for health communication.

Our Approach

Two-Part Evaluation Framework

Analysis 1Back-Translation Fidelity — Translate English → Target Language → Back to English. Compare back-translation to original. Measures how well the LLM preserves meaning through round-trip translation.

Analysis 2Professional Comparison — Compare LLM translation directly to professional human translation (same target language). Measures how closely LLM output matches expert human work.

ValidationMethodology Check — Back-translate professional translations through each LLM. High fidelity (LaBSE 0.92-0.94) confirms back-translation is a valid evaluation method.

Understanding the Metrics

LaBSE
Semantic similarity (0-1). Measures meaning preservation regardless of exact words. Most important for health content.
BLEU
Word/phrase overlap (0-100). Measures exact wording similarity. Lower for non-Latin scripts—doesn't indicate worse translations.
BERTScore
Contextual similarity (0-1). Balances meaning and phrasing.
COMET
Neural translation quality (0-1). Trained on human judgments. Most aligned with human preferences.

Results by Language

The critical finding: semantic preservation (LaBSE) is consistent across all resource levels.

Why are Chinese/Korean BLEU scores low? BLEU counts word matches, but these languages use different tokenization and word boundaries. The semantic metrics (LaBSE 0.94) confirm meaning is preserved just as well—BLEU is simply less appropriate for these scripts.

Low-resource languages hold their own. Tagalog and Haitian Creole achieve semantic scores comparable to Spanish and Vietnamese. LLMs can reliably translate medical content for historically underserved language communities.

Key Takeaways

  • LLMs preserve medical meaning reliably across all tested languages — Semantic similarity scores (LaBSE 0.92-0.98) indicate core health information is maintained.
  • Resource level doesn't determine translation quality — Low-resource languages (Tagalog, Haitian Creole) achieve semantic fidelity comparable to high-resource languages (Spanish, Vietnamese).
  • Semantic vs lexical metrics tell different stories — BLEU varies by script and morphology; LaBSE measures what matters for health communication: meaning preservation.
  • Claude Opus 4.5 excels at faithful translation — Best at preserving exact meaning through round-trip translation.
  • Gemini 3 Pro writes most like human translators — Closest stylistic match to professional translations.
  • Model selection matters less than expected — All frontier models perform within a narrow band, suggesting convergence in translation capability.

Implications for Health Equity

These findings have significant implications for medical translation access:

  • LLMs could democratize medical translation for speakers of low-resource languages where professional translation services are unavailable or delayed.
  • Meaning preservation is reliable — patients receive accurate health information regardless of their language's "resource level."
  • LLM translation should complement, not replace, professional translation for high-stakes clinical documents. Our results support LLM use for patient education materials where general comprehension is the goal.

Study Details

Models Tested

  • Claude Opus 4.5 (Anthropic)
  • GPT-5.1 (OpenAI)
  • Gemini 3 Pro (Google)
  • Kimi K2 (Moonshot AI)

Languages

  • High-resource (>1% CommonCrawl): Spanish, Chinese, Vietnamese, Russian
  • Medium-resource (0.1-1%): Korean, Arabic
  • Low-resource (<0.1%): Tagalog, Haitian Creole

Documents

  • 11 CDC Vaccine Information Statements (via Immunize.org)
  • 11 American Cancer Society patient education materials
  • All with existing professional translations in all 8 languages
